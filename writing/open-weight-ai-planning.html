Essay / AI & local government

Whose machine is it running on?

Irish councils are adopting generative AI mostly through Microsoft Copilot. For planning departments, which hold some of the most personal data in local government, open-weight models on infrastructure the public sector controls deserve a serious look, provided we are honest about what openness does and does not buy.

Dean Brickland 9 October 2026 9 min read

Every planning department in Ireland already has a generative AI policy, whether or not anyone has written it down. If the council does not provide a sanctioned tool, staff under pressure will find an unsanctioned one, and observations, enforcement complaints and draft reports will end up pasted into a free chatbot somewhere. The Government’s Guidelines for the Responsible Use of AI in the Public Service recognise the risk. They advise against free or public generative AI tools, and recommend that staff access be restricted by default until a business case, a usage policy and an awareness programme are in place.

So the question is not whether councils adopt AI but which kind, and on whose terms. The default answer is Microsoft Copilot, because councils already run on Microsoft 365. Dublin City Council held seven Copilot licences by June 2025, according to records released under Freedom of Information. That default is reasonable. My argument is that for planning work it should not be the only option tested, and that open-weight models, which can be downloaded and run on infrastructure the council or the sector controls, belong in the comparison.

Three decisions, not one

Public comparisons of AI products usually compare unlike things. Choosing an AI service is really three decisions. The first is the application staff use: the interface, sign-in, file access, history and audit. The second is the model that generates the text, with its own licence, provenance and update policy. The third is deployment: who runs the model, where prompts and documents are processed and stored, and who patches, monitors and supports it. Copilot bundles all three. An open-weight model is only the second, and it does not arrive as a finished assistant.

The distinction matters because the claims made for each option attach to different layers. Downloadable weights are not, on their own, sovereignty, GDPR compliance or security; those depend on how and where the model is deployed. Equally, a well-known enterprise assistant does not guarantee that every feature runs in the same region under the same terms. The question in the title is the deployment question, and for planning it is the one that cannot be skipped.

Why planning is different

Planning departments hold an unusual mix of data. Much of what we work with is public by design: development plans, ministerial guidelines, the National Planning Framework, decisions and inspectors’ reports. Many useful early applications, such as searching policy, checking a proposal against a plan’s objectives or summarising a long circular, need nothing more.

But the planning file is also full of personal data. Applications carry names and addresses, and sometimes details of health or finances offered in support of a case. Every observation and every development plan submission comes from an identifiable person. Enforcement files contain complaints about neighbours. This is where AI could save the most time. In England, PlanAI, built by Greater Cambridge Shared Planning and the University of Liverpool, cut the analysis of one set of local plan consultation responses from around 18.5 hours to about 16 minutes. The UK government’s Consult tool, whose code is open source, has been used on 38 consultations; in one Department for Work and Pensions exercise, reviewers left its suggested themes unchanged for 73% of responses.

Here a familiar safeguard breaks down. Many AI pilots handle data protection by banning personal data from the tool. That is the right rule for a first trial, but in planning it rules out the most valuable work. A department that wants help with submissions has to decide where personal data will be processed, by whom and under which legal regime.

What Copilot offers

Copilot deserves a fair hearing. It works inside Outlook, Word, Teams and SharePoint, where staff already spend their day. Microsoft acts as a processor under contract, states that business data is not used to train its foundation models, and keeps customer data within its EU Data Boundary. Copilot inherits the council’s existing permissions, security and records tools, and the council runs no infrastructure. Three things still need checking.

Data location is a set of settings, not a single guarantee. Microsoft’s own documentation lists exceptions to the EU Data Boundary. Web searches that Copilot generates go to Bing outside the Boundary and Microsoft’s data protection terms. Anthropic’s models, now offered inside Copilot, are excluded from it, though they are off by default for EU customers. And “flex routing”, which lets processing move outside the EU at times of peak demand, has been on by default for eligible tenants created after 25 March 2026; administrators can turn it off. Ireland is not among the countries where Microsoft offers in-country processing, so “local” means somewhere in the EU, not here.

Jurisdiction. Microsoft is a US company, and transfers of personal data to the United States rest on the EU–US Data Privacy Framework. The General Court upheld it in September 2025, but the ruling is under appeal to the Court of Justice, which struck down the framework’s two predecessors in 2015 and 2020.

Transparency and permissions. An impact assessment for the Dutch government in December 2024 found four high data protection risks in Microsoft 365 Copilot; the Dutch education body SURF reported in September 2025 that two had been reduced to medium after negotiation. That took a national government’s leverage, which no Irish council has alone. And Copilot surfaces anything the user is already permitted to see, so where SharePoint permissions have grown untidy over a decade, it will find documents nobody realised were widely shared.

What open-weight models offer

Open-weight models can be downloaded and run on servers the council chooses. Most are not “open source” in the Open Source Initiative’s sense, which also requires training code and detailed information about the training data. A few come close: Ai2’s Olmo 3 and Apertus 1.5, a Swiss public-interest model from ETH Zurich and EPFL, publish weights, code and data details under Apache 2.0. For Irish-language work, Qomhrá, an Irish–English model from researchers at Trinity, UCC and Queen’s University Belfast, is an early sign of home-grown capability.

Licences must be read model by model, and version by version. Mistral’s Small and Ministral models, Alibaba’s Qwen3.8-27B, Google’s Gemma 4 and OpenAI’s gpt-oss are released under Apache 2.0. But Mistral’s Medium 3.5 carries a modified licence that withdraws rights above a revenue threshold, and Meta’s Llama 4 policy withholds its multimodal licence rights from companies domiciled in the EU, which a council would need legal advice on before downloading it.

For a planning department, the benefit is control of the deployment layer. If a model runs in an Irish or EU data centre operated by or for the public sector, submissions never leave infrastructure under public control. There is no third-country transfer to justify and no supplier routing setting to monitor. The council decides what is logged and retained, which matters for Freedom of Information, and it fixes the version, so the model cannot change or disappear halfway through a development plan review. Ireland’s National Cyber Security Centre now expects this comparison to be made: its June 2026 guidance on securing AI adoption in the public sector asks whether cloud, on-premises and open-source or open-weight options have been evaluated against the use case.

The costs are real, though. A zero licence fee is not a zero-cost service: compute, engineering, security testing and assurance replace the per-seat price, which for Microsoft 365 Copilot lists at US$30 per user per month. The best open models still trail commercial systems on some tasks, and the integration Copilot provides would have to be built. Provenance matters too. The European Data Protection Board’s Opinion 28/2024, requested by the Irish Data Protection Commission, says that unlawful processing when a model was trained can affect the lawfulness of using it later.

A shared service, not 31 experiments

No council should do this alone. Running models well needs GPU capacity, security engineering and evaluation skills that few authorities have, and that is the strongest argument for treating open-weight AI as shared infrastructure. Ireland has a starting point. The AI Factory Antenna, launched at ICHEC in July 2026 with €10 million, lists the public sector among its audiences, although its access process has not been published. I have found no shared generative AI service for local government yet, but the sector already runs national planning systems together, and its Digital and ICT Strategy 2030 says AI must come with appropriate governance and ethical arrangements.

Elsewhere in Europe this already works. France runs Albert, an interministerial AI service built on its MIT-licensed OpenGateLLM gateway, which other public bodies can host themselves. Baden-Württemberg has open-sourced F13, its administration assistant. Denmark offers a caution: of 40 earlier public-sector AI projects, 22 were completed and only 6 fully implemented, and most ran into data and GDPR problems. Shared scale is a funding and governance decision, not a shortcut around the hard work.

Rules for either route

  • Classify before you choose. Public documents, personal data from the planning process and sensitive files such as enforcement cases and legal advice carry different risks, and may justify different tools.
  • Test on your own work. Build 30 to 50 real tasks from published plans and reports, time them without AI first, and score every option on the same set for accuracy, citations, correction time and whether it admits when the answer is not in the source. Public leaderboards measure nothing a planner does.
  • Keep the decision and the authorship human. A planning decision has legal effects, and GDPR limits solely automated decisions. AI output is a draft; the officer who signs the report remains its author.
  • Plan for the AI Act. The Regulation of Artificial Intelligence Act 2026 was signed in July, and the AI Office of Ireland was established at the end of that month. The EU’s AI Omnibus has moved the main high-risk obligations to December 2027, but the transparency duties already apply. Most uses described here are unlikely to be high-risk; anything close to assessing an application should be treated as if it were.
  • Publish what you use. A public register of the AI tools used in the planning system would do more for trust than any supplier’s assurance.

The question to ask

Copilot is a sensible tool for the general office work every department does, and most councils will use it. But planning departments hold the personal data of everyone who has applied for permission or objected to a neighbour’s extension. For that work, the most useful question to ask of any AI tool is a plain one: whose machine is it running on? Open-weight models on public infrastructure are the option where the answer can be “ours”, but only if the sector builds that option together and checks the licence, the model and the deployment as rigorously as it would check Copilot.

This is a personal view. It does not represent the position of any organisation I work with, including the Irish Planning Institute, and it is not legal advice. Sources were checked in September and October 2026; product settings and licences change often.
